Reward-context inputs to SC on the corrected exports: what the RNN fits can and cannot tell apart

Corrected exports (pipeline 1.2.0), Feynman 08_12–08_15 and Newton 08_13–08_15. Built 2026-10-15 from commit 204d64f by scripts/make_context_report.py. Prepared by Claude for James Herman. Supersedes the conclusions of results/recovery_pilot_report.html.

Before the target appears, SC neurons fire slightly more when their preferred location currently gives the large reward. We asked whether E–I network models need a separate reward-context input for each neuron to reproduce which neurons show this effect, or whether one shared reward signal, routed by the local circuit, would do.

On interleaved test trials, per-unit models appeared to reproduce the pattern (r = 0.47), but a control with context shifted out of register scored almost as well (0.35): the score mostly reflected slow fluctuations shared between training and test trials. Holding out whole reward switches removes that leak. The neuron-by-neuron pattern then barely repeats from switch to switch (ceiling 0.21), aligned context gains only about 0.1 over its controls, and shared and per-unit context fit equally well (+0.04 ± 0.05). With idealized teachers the procedure separates the mechanisms clearly (shared minus per-unit -0.28 and -0.23 for per-unit teachers, +0.10 for a shared one). With teachers as noisy as the data, it no longer does: the contrast is negative for every teacher, -0.19 and -0.12 for per-unit teachers but also -0.16 for the shared teacher and -0.08 with no context at all. Held-out likelihood separates the two kinds of teacher only weakly. With the present number of reward switches, these fits cannot decide whether SC reward-context input is neuron-specific or a shared signal routed by the local circuit; the data favour neither.

2. The population effect is real but small, and comes from two sessions

FPre-target firing rose by +0.28 sp/s on average when a neuron's preferred location became the large-reward location (30 trials after vs before each switch; p = 0.0007 across 105 switches). The effect is significant in Newton 08_13 and 08_14 only (Fig. 1). Newton 08_14 has the most slow drift of any session; the switch-triggered contrast is local, but conclusions resting on that session alone should be treated with caution.

3. Models

SEach model is an E–I rate network with one unit per recorded neuron, Dale's law and local-only inhibition. It receives the task events, eye position, three slow-drift inputs, and (except the no-context variant) two context inputs: the reward map and the probability block. Variants differ only in how the context reaches the units (Fig. 2). Each variant was fit 6 times from different random starts ("restarts").

4. An interleaved test set overstates what the models capture

FWith test trials scattered through the session, per-unit models reproduced the neuron-by-neuron pattern well (r = 0.47 for per-unit → all; ceiling 0.55; Fig. 3). But a control whose context input was shifted a quarter cycle out of register with the real context reached 0.35. That shifted input carries no reward information at the switches, so most of the score came from something else: per-unit inputs let each unit follow its neuron's slow fluctuations on the training trials, and those fluctuations are shared with the neighbouring test trials. Shared-context models (0.36) scored about as high as the shifted control.

5. Holding out whole switches: the pattern is weak, and shared and per-unit context are indistinguishable

SWe refit every variant holding out whole windows of ±30 trials around 2 random reward switches for testing (and 2 for checkpoint selection), different switches for each restart. A test switch then shares no slow fluctuations with training trials, and any effect a model predicts there must come from its context input.

FThe ceiling dropped from 0.55 to 0.21: measured across switches, each neuron's reward-context effect is only weakly reproducible. Against this ceiling, models with aligned context scored 0.16–0.21, the shifted controls 0.12 and 0.10, and no context 0.14. The no-context score is not zero, because which trials fall in the test windows already shapes the measured effect. Paired over restarts (Fig. 5), aligned context beats its shifted control by about 0.1, within 1–2 standard errors, and shared minus per-unit is +0.04 ± 0.05.

6. Recovery test: can the procedure tell a shared from a per-unit mechanism?

STeachers are networks already fit to each session, whose mechanism is therefore known: no context, shared context (gain set so that its context-driven effects are as large as the per-unit teacher's), per-unit context to all units, and per-unit context to I units only. Spikes were generated from each teacher on the real trials, and students of every variant were fit to them exactly as to the real data, with the switch-window split.

FWith idealized teachers the procedure works. No student found a reward pattern in the no-context teacher (ceiling -0.00). Each context teacher was recovered best by its own kind of student, and shared minus per-unit students separated the mechanisms in every restart: -0.28 and -0.23 for the per-unit teachers, +0.10 for the shared teacher (Figs. 6, 8). A shared signal also produced a neuron-specific pattern that per-unit students fit well (0.50), so a good per-unit fit is not by itself evidence for per-unit input.

FBut these teachers are easier than the data. Their ceilings (0.47–0.64) are far above the recordings', because a teacher's context effect is the same at every switch, while recorded neurons also fluctuate slowly for other reasons. We therefore multiplied each teacher's rates by its recorded neuron's own slow rate fluctuation (smoothed over about 15 trials, mean preserved), shifted by whole reward cycles plus a quarter cycle so that it carries no reward-context signal, with a strength per teacher chosen so that its ceiling matches the recordings.

FWith these realistic teachers (ceilings 0.19–0.23), the contrast loses its power (Figs. 7, 8). Shared minus per-unit was -0.19 ± 0.06 and -0.12 ± 0.07 for the per-unit teachers, but -0.16 ± 0.06 for the shared teacher and -0.08 ± 0.05 for the no-context teacher. Per-unit students now score higher whatever the mechanism: their extra freedom lets them follow fluctuations at the held-out switches that are unrelated to context. Even with no context in the teacher, per-unit students scored 0.24 against a ceiling of -0.01, so scores on held-out switches are biased upwards at this noise level.

7. Fit quality

FThe models fit trial-averaged responses well where the recordings are reliable (Fig. 9: median r 0.60 against a median split-half reliability of 0.52 over 270 units; r and reliability correlate across units). Visual selectivity for location is reproduced on held-out trials (Fig. 10: r 0.57, range 0.32–0.84 over restarts); identity, salience and probability selectivity are not.

8. Conclusions and open problems

  • Reward context modulates SC pre-target activity weakly (+0.28 sp/s), mainly in two Newton sessions.
  • The neuron-by-neuron pattern is not reliably measurable across switches with these data (ceiling 0.21). Earlier, higher reliability came from slow fluctuations, not context.
  • Shared and per-unit context fit the real data equally well; the earlier conclusion that only per-unit context reproduces the pattern is withdrawn.
  • With data-like noise, the fitting procedure cannot tell shared from per-unit context. On teachers whose reliability matches the recordings, per-unit students win the held-out-r contrast whatever the teacher's mechanism, and held-out likelihood separates mechanisms only weakly. The earlier reading that the real data match the shared prediction is withdrawn: the data favour neither.
  • Scores on held-out switches are biased upwards at this noise level (per-unit students score about 0.2 on a teacher with no context), so they should be compared against matched synthetic controls, not against zero or the ceiling.
